Two power domains that share only a ground, and one data path. The Roomba keeps its own battery and its own charger exactly as they are; everything you add hangs off the LiPo. The only wire between the two worlds is a serial cable.

FIG 2Why the pack feeds the Jetson directly

This is the piece of luck that simplifies the whole build. The Xavier NX carrier accepts 9–20 V on its barrel jack — it merely ships with a 19 V brick. A 3S LiPo lives between 10.5 V and 12.6 V. That range sits entirely inside the Jetson's window, so the pack wires straight to the barrel with no converter and no conversion losses.

Charge to 12.0 V, not 12.6 V

A docked robot sits at whatever voltage you charge it to, for days at a time. Holding a LiPo at 4.2 V/cell is what makes packs puff and lose capacity in months. Setting your buck's CV point to 12.0 V (4.0 V/cell) costs about 17% of runtime — 4.4 h down to 3.6 h — and buys several times the cycle life. On a robot that lives on a dock, this is the highest-value setting on the whole build.

FIG 3Where the watchdog lives

The tempting shortcut is to leave the control loop on your Mac and bridge the serial port over Wi-Fi. Don't — it puts the safety timer on the wrong side of the radio.

WIRINGConnections

The serial side is already solved — your existing FTDI cable handles pins 3, 4 and 6. The only optional addition is the wake line, and it wants a 2N3904 with a 10 kΩ base resistor rather than a MOSFET: a BJT has no gate-threshold margin to worry about on 3.3 V GPIO, where even a 2N7000 can need up to 3 V, and it is through-hole.

Four things this topology does not protect against

Two wires land on P+, so both need a fuse. The load branch and the charge branch are separate external connections to the same pad, and a single fuse in the load leg leaves the whole charge path — pogo pins, buck, Schottky — covered only by the buck’s 2 A limit and the BMS’s 34–40 A short trip. A Schottky that fails shorted can then pull pack current toward that ceiling through 18 AWG. Hence a fuse in each leg above; the holders come four to a pack, so this costs nothing.

The fuse is 3 A, not 5. NVIDIA’s carrier spec caps the DC jack at 3.5 A (§3.8, and DCJ_IN in Table 5‑2). A 5 A fuse passes a sustained 3.5–5 A fault indefinitely while cooking the connector it terminates in.

Nothing watches the charge current. The INA219 is a high-side shunt in the discharge leg, so it never sees the 2 A coming in. A buck drifting high or a degrading Schottky has no monitor at all and falls straight to the BMS’s 4.25–4.35 V/cell over-voltage trip as the only backstop — no timeout, no drift detection. Its ±3.2 A range also sits below the jack’s 3.5 A, so a genuine peak clips the reading exactly when you would want it accurate.

Nothing protects the buck’s input. The Schottky is on its output; the 19 V input from the dock pads has no series diode, PMOS or fuse, and generic MP2304 modules have no input reverse-polarity protection. Exposed contacts are exactly where reversed polarity happens. Worth solving as part of the dock rework, which is on hold anyway.

Two smaller ones, both unverified for this exact hardware. The 1N5822 dissipates about 1 W at 2 A and is an inline part in a closed housing with no airflow specified — give it air, or use a bigger diode. And floating at 4.0 V/cell may never reach the voltage at which a cheap bleed-balance board starts balancing (typically ~4.15 V), so a robot that lives on the dock could drift out of balance with nothing correcting it. That threshold is not published for this board; check cell spread by hand at the balance pads every few months.

Setting up the DROK converter

The DROK 6–32 V → 1.5–32 V 5 A module (MP2304) does constant current as well as constant voltage, so it is the charger — no separate charge board needed. At 19 V in and 12.0 V / 2.0 A out it runs at 24 W of its 75 W ceiling, with 7 V of headroom over its 1.5 V minimum dropout.

Three cautions specific to it. It ships preset to about 20 V, so wind the output down before the pack is ever connected. Calibrate against a multimeter rather than its LCD, measuring at the pack terminals — the ~0.4 V drop across the output Schottky means the display reads about 12.45 V when the pack sees 12.0 V. And it holds its CV point indefinitely rather than terminating, which is precisely why the target is 12.0 V and not 12.6 V: floating at 4.0 V/cell is benign, floating at 4.2 V/cell is what kills packs.

The frame bolts to the chassis, which means the faceplate comes off

Six chassis screw bosses carry the whole frame, two per carrier, and they sit under the faceplate. Pulling or drilling it is not optional. The VHB tape route is gone for good — the hub used to carry four 18 mm pad recesses on its underside so it could be pressed on with no holes at all, and those recesses no longer exist in the model.

Nobody has measured those bosses. They are almost certainly self-tapping into plastic rather than M3, in which case the standoffs bolt to each other and the original faceplate screws go into the bosses. Pull one screw and look before printing anything — six hole sizes depend on the answer.

Two traps worth naming

Not every "3S BMS with balance" protects against over-discharge. A popular 40 A board carries a verified review reporting no undervoltage cutoff at all — the reviewer fed 2 V per cell and it discharged freely. On a board whose only job is protection, that is disqualifying. The board above publishes its thresholds, which is the thing to check before buying any substitute.

5.5×2.1 mm is the wrong barrel size and dominates the search results, including a listing with 1.7K reviews. The Xavier NX needs 5.5×2.5 mm, centre positive.

Contact geometry

Off-the-shelf pogo connectors are the wrong part here, and not only because the pads are small. A 4-pin block at 2.5 mm pitch spans 7.5 mm end to end while the robot docks to about ±10 mm — the tolerance is wider than the whole connector. Worse, with both polarities inside 7.5 mm, one oversized pad would bridge them and short the 19 V rail. Individual pins, spread out, fix both problems at once.

Pins go on the dock pointing up, so the energised metal is small and hard to bridge; the pads go on the robot, dead whenever it is undocked. Cut them from the copper-clad board, keep them clear of the cliff sensors, and solder the wire straight to each pin's tail.

Deliberately not on the list

The 2N3904 + 10 kΩ for the BRC wake line, which is optional — without it you press CLEAN once per session, which is the status quo anyway. A BJT beats the BSS138 here: no gate-threshold margin problem on 3.3 V GPIO, and it is through-hole. Extra 16 AWG wire too — the fuse holders ship with pigtails that may already be long enough.

Mounting to the robot

The frame is a hub ring around the Clean button with three carriers — Jetson on the right, camera and buck at the front, pack on the left — and deliberately nothing at all behind the hub. Each carrier laps onto a hub pad, steps down 6 mm, and then lies flat on the deck for the whole of its length. That step is the entire design: in the previous revision every payload was stacked on top of an arm, and getting them back down onto the shell is where most of the height went.

Each carrier also bolts to two chassis screw bosses of its own. The OI spec’s page 6 gives the positions; the six in use are (76, +50.5) and (113.5, −78) for the Jetson plate, (±30, +127) for the front plate, and (−109, +88) and (−51.8, −66) for the cradle. They are under the 690’s faceplate, so using them still means pulling or drilling it.

The hub is not bolted to the robot at all — it is a tie, not a mount. The three carriers bolt down onto its pads and the M3 nuts sit in hex pockets in the pads’ undersides, trapped between the hub and the Roomba’s shell. Nothing in the frame hangs on a thread cut in printed plastic.

What the low-deck rewrite bought

Peak height fell from about 207 mm to 125.2 mm, against a stock robot of 92.25. The camera’s optical centre fell from about 194 mm to 110 mm. Of the 82 mm, 71 came from deleting the mast: the D435i now sits in a friction-clamp rocker whose pivot runs through its own optical centre, so tilting aims the camera without moving it, and the 25 mm body is the only thing standing above the pivot. The remaining 11 came from putting the carriers on the deck instead of on arms.

The tallest thing on the robot is now the INA219 on the battery lid at 125.2 mm, with the Jetson’s fan at 122.7 just under it. So there is one more millimetre-scale move available — take the two boards off the lid and the build drops to 122.7 — but the lid is where they electrically belong, 40 mm from the cells, and 2.5 mm is not worth a longer balance lead. Below that, the Jetson’s own 34.66 mm stack is the floor.

Six distinct printed parts replaced seven, and because four of the old ones were identical arms the piece count went from ten to six.

It also fixed the balance. The old layout hung a 424 g pack off one arm against a 74 g electronics row on the other — a standing wheel-load difference that the encoder odometry reads as a heading bias. Now the left group (cradle, pack, lid, BMS, INA219) is 491 g at x −88 and the right group (plate and Jetson) 318 g at x +109, and they very nearly cancel: verify.py weighs the placed geometry and puts the 988 g payload’s centre of mass 8.6 mm left of the centreline, which is 1.9 mm once the 3.5 kg robot is counted. Tipping was never the concern and still is not — 235 mm of track against a centre of mass near 57 mm gives a threshold around 64°.

And the dust bin comes out again. The old Jetson plate sat squarely over the release at (0, −120); nothing in this layout goes aft of y −38 except the hub. The rear deck now carries only the rocker switch and the two fuse holders, both of which hang on their own leads and can be reached with a fingertip.

110 mm is derived, not chosen

Low is only free until the robot starts occluding its own near field, and raycasting the reference CAD showed exactly one thing standing above the deck to occlude with: a 16 × 18 mm boss on the front centreline, spanning y 147–165 at z 90.94, with flat 83 mm deck either side of it. That boss is what the OI spec’s 92.25 mm “height” actually measures, and on a 600-series it is the omnidirectional dock and virtual-wall IR receiver.

From an optical centre at (0, 142.5, 110) the grazing ray over that boss leaves at 39.7° below horizontal and meets the floor 132 mm ahead — a slant range of 172 mm, which is inside the D435i’s own Min-Z of 175–195 mm at VGA and 848 × 480. So the boss costs no usable depth at all: everything it hides was already closer than the camera can measure. Off the centreline the horizon is the 83 mm body edge, grazing at 49.6° for a 144 mm slant, also inside Min-Z.

Drop to 105 mm and the centre ray breaks even with Min-Z. Drop to 100 and a wedge of near floor about 27° wide goes blind behind the boss. Raise it and you buy nothing — closer than Min-Z the camera is blind for its own reasons, and the Jetson sets the build height regardless. 110 mm is the floor of that window with a little margin.

Still unverified, and still the most consequential thing on this page

That front boss is identified from geometry, not from looking at the robot. It is the only feature above deck level, it is on the centreline immediately behind the bumper, and that is where a 600-series carries its IR receiver — but confirm it by eye before printing the front plate. If the frame ever covers that receiver the robot cannot find its dock, which takes the entire charge rail with it. The front plate stops at y 144, 3 mm short of it, and nothing on the plate stands in front of it; the camera and the DROK do sit behind and above it, which narrows the acquisition cone from the rear. Any payload does that, and the robot spins to search, so it is a cost worth naming rather than a fault.

The 690’s carry handle is also still unrecorded. The Create 2 CAD this build measures does not have one, so nothing here is placed around it. Look along the front centreline before committing.

And the dust bin line is inferred, not measured. Everything is kept forward of y −78 because the chassis bosses at (0, −78) and (±113.5, −78) are almost certainly the rail at the bin’s front edge. Check that the bin actually slides out with the frame on before you trust it.

Where the geometry comes from. Printed parts are drawn from dimensions.json and exported to STL. Bought parts are datasheet envelopes, with one exception: the D435i is Intel's own model, taken from the mesh Intel ships in the RealSense ROS package and measured at 89.91 × 25.00 × 25.06 mm against the datasheet's 90 × 25 × 25. Intel also publishes SolidWorks parts for the D400 series, but nothing free on macOS reads that format. The whole Jetson developer kit is NVIDIA’s own CAD — carrier, module, connectors, heatsink and fan. It measures 100.00 × 79.00 × 1.57 mm at the carrier and 34.66 mm over the stack, both exact to the carrier board specification, which is how we know the file is genuine. Two things it corrected: the fan is recessed into the 57.8 × 39 × 18 mm heatsink rather than stacked on it, and the 103 × 90.5 mm figure NVIDIA publishes is a keep-out envelope, not the board. The Roomba and the Home Base have no published CAD at all and are the next thing to scan.

The body, and a mounting height correction. The Roomba shell is now the Create 2 reference CAD from GrabCAD. The Create 2 is a remanufactured 600-series chassis, so it is the 690’s shell. It measures 345.19 × 339.18 × 90.95 mm against the spec’s 348.5 × 339.2 × 92.25: fore-aft exact, deck height within 0.1 mm, width 3.3 mm under. Good to a few millimetres, so it is the display shape while the dimension table stays authoritative for fit.

It caught a real error, and then corrected the correction. The frame was originally being mounted at 92.25 mm, the height quoted in the OI spec; that figure is the maximum, and the deck the frame actually lies on is flat at 82.94 mm. The first fix attributed the extra 9 mm to “a raised section about 50 mm deep at the very front”, and that was a guess. Raycasting the CAD on a 2 mm grid says the deck is flat at 83.01 continuously from y −116 all the way to y +144, and the only thing above it anywhere on the robot is a 16 × 18 mm boss on the front centreline at z 90.94 — the IR receiver. There is no raised front section. That matters: the camera bracket was previously described as cantilevered onto a slope that does not exist, and the real constraint turned out to be optical rather than mechanical.

One number to watch: the same CAD puts the shell underside 10.9 mm above the floor, where the dock design assumes 8 mm. More clearance means the pogo pins must reach further. Do the card-stack measurement before printing the pad carrier.

Setting the pin height. The front caster rides up the Home Base ramp, so at the dock the pads sit roughly 9 mm above the local ramp surface, and a P75-B1 only has 2.5 mm of travel. The heights in the model are nominal: the real ramp profile is not in any datasheet. Fit the pins last. Dock the robot, measure from the ramp surface to the copper, then press each pin into the block until its tip sits 1.5 mm above that height and lock it with a drop of CA. If the ramp plate is thinner than the pin body, drill 1.6 mm through and let the pins pass into the hollow beneath. The 3D view draws the robot nose-up on the ramp with the block on the slope under the pads, which is the pose to measure in.

The dock contact design is on hold

A dedicated search for the Home Base’s dimensions confirmed the 149 × 159 × 114 mm envelope against three independent retail listings, but found that two of my ramp numbers were wrong. The sloped apron is roughly 75 mm deep, not the 110 mm assumed, and the stock contacts are about 80 mm apart, not 60. Both came from scaled product photography and manual line art, so treat them as estimates with wide error bars: 60–90 mm and 70–90 mm respectively.

That shorter ramp breaks the pad placement. Docked, the ramp now spans y 99.9 to 174.9, so pads at y=100 sit on the front lip where the ramp surface is at zero height and the gap to the robot’s underside is the full ~11 mm. Sliding the pads further up the ramp runs them into the front caster at y=120. There is no arrangement that works on paper with the numbers we have.

So do not print the pad carrier or the pin block yet. Two measurements unblock it: one level, side-on photo of the Home Base against a ruler to get the true ramp rise and depth, and the card-stack test for the clearance under the docked robot. Everything else in this build can proceed meanwhile.

Two numbers the drawings corrected

The Jetson dev kit is 100 × 79 mm of PCB inside a 103 × 90.5 mm envelope, only 34.66 mm tall with the stock heatsink and fan — both figures appear in NVIDIA's mechanicals and they measure different things. The Roomba body is Ø329.9 with a Ø348.5 bumper, 92.25 mm tall.

ORDERBuild sequence

Each step leaves you with something testable, so a mistake is caught while it is still cheap to find.

  1. Jetson on the bench, robot on the floor

    Run ./run.sh --host 0.0.0.0 on the Jetson with the FTDI cable and mains power. Drive it from your Mac's browser. This proves the whole software path before any wiring exists.

  2. Look at the robot, then print

    Faceplate off. Find the six chassis bosses and check what thread they take; find the 16 × 18 mm boss on the front centreline and confirm it is the IR receiver; check whether there is a carry handle in that band; and pull the dust bin out to see where its front edge really is. Four things, one look, and every one of them can force a reprint.

    Then print the hub and the three carriers and dry-fit them with the payloads in place but nothing wired. Run python3 hardware/verify.py first if you have changed any dimension — it re-assembles the exported STLs and checks height, reach, interference, the tilt sweep and the four keep-outs.

  3. Pack protection first

    The BMS lives on the battery lid, directly over the cells, so this is all short wire. Pack onto the BMS — T-plug to B+/B−, the four balance wires to B−, B1, B2, B+ — then everything downstream hangs off P+: the 3 A fuse, then the switch. Do this before the pack ever powers anything.

    Do not try to prove the cutoff by discharging the pack. 10.5 V is the software threshold, not the BMS’s: this board trips at 2.3–3.0 V per cell, so around 8.4 V at the pack — and running a new 8 Ah LiPo that flat to watch a protection circuit work damages the thing you are protecting. There is also no load wired at this step. Prove the wiring instead: meter each cell at the balance pads to confirm the taps are in cell order and none is shorted, check P+ to P− reads pack voltage, then briefly short P+ to P− and confirm it latches off and recovers. That exercises the protection without abusing the cells.

  4. Cut the cord

    BMS P+ → fuse → switch → Jetson barrel. Confirm it boots and holds voltage under load with the RealSense streaming. Measure the pack at idle and at full tilt.

  5. Charge rail on the bench

    With the pack disconnected, wind the DROK down from its ~20 V factory preset and set 12.0 V / 2.0 A, verified with a meter at the pack end of the Schottky. Then charge once by hand and watch the current taper. Lock the trimmers before it goes near the dock.

  6. Dock contacts last

    Blocked — do not print or build this step yet. The ramp depth correction breaks the current pad placement; see “The dock contact design is on hold” above. Everything below is the intended design, pending the two measurements.

    Pogo pins on the dock base pointing up, flat pads on the robot's underside — the robot's weight makes the contact. Skip magnetic connectors: this robot drives in and out under its own power, so retention magnets only fight undocking. Double up a 4-pin block (two pins each for + and −) so no single 2 A pin carries the whole charge current, and keep the pads clear of the cliff sensors.

  7. Pack telemetry and the software cutoff

    INA219 onto I2C pins 27/28. Surface pack voltage in the control panel next to the Roomba's own battery, warn at 11.0 V, and trigger a graceful shutdown at 10.5 V. None of this exists in the code yet — it is the first item on the software backlog below, and until it is written the BMS is the only thing under the pack.

  8. Optional: the wake line

    Jetson GPIO → 2N3904 + 10 kΩ → pin 5. Pulse low for 1 s to wake the robot remotely and retire the CLEAN button press.

SAFETYBefore it lives on a dock

  • A fuse on each external connection to P+, load and charge both — 50C × 8 Ah is a 400 A short-circuit potential.
  • BMS wired to the balance connector and feeding the load from P+, not B+. Taps on B+ bypass the protection entirely.
  • Buck confirmed to current-limit, set to 12.0 V / 2.0 A.
  • Low-voltage cutoff done in software at 10.5 V, not with a relay — see below.
  • The soft-case pack in a rigid enclosure, clear of the bumper's travel. This robot drives into things for a living.
  • Second rail on an isolated supply; grounds bonded at one point on the robot only.
  • Dock sited on a hard floor, away from soft furnishings.
  • Nothing connected to mini-DIN pins 1 and 2.

Cut off in software, not with a relay

The obvious part here is a low-voltage disconnect module, and it is the wrong answer twice over. The common ones (XH-M609 and its clones) are rated DC 12–36 V — below their own operating floor at the 10.5 V you want them to act on. And a relay cutting power to a running Linux box risks corrupting the filesystem every time the battery gets low.

Instead: an INA219 on the Jetson's I2C header reads pack voltage and current. Warn at 11.0 V in the control panel, shutdown -h now at 10.5 V. The BMS stays underneath as a hard backstop at its stock ~2.8 V/cell, firing only if the software failed — which means an ordinary cheap BMS is exactly the right part, with no exotic cutoff needed.

The one thing this design doesn't remove

Even done correctly, this is a soft-case LiPo charging unattended in your home. The BMS, the fuse and the conservative charge voltage bring it to roughly the risk profile of charging an e-bike, which is a reasonable thing to accept knowingly.

A 4S LiFePO4 with an integrated BMS drops into exactly this design — same voltage window, same dock, same wiring — and removes the category entirely. Worth knowing the swap exists if you ever stop wanting to think about it.
